# history-plot-twists: fact-check log

Checked by an independent checker on 2026-11-08 against DECK_SPEC.md and CHECK_SPEC.md.

## Changes

- hp01: the cap "Marco Polo brought noodles home to Italy from China in 1295" isn't strictly provable as false (whether he carried noodles is unknown). The `real` claim that the myth "came from a 1929 pasta trade magazine" couldn't be confirmed: the Tampa Bay source is an empty archive page, and Lapham's Quarterly only says the story was "probably invented" by the US macaroni makers' association. → Cap reworded to "Marco Polo brought pasta to Italy for the first time in 1295" (definitely false). `real` now uses the documented record: a Genoa soldier's 1279 inventory listing a basket of macaroni (Lapham's Quarterly). Source replaced. Also checked: Vikings felling trees in 1021 (Science News), Ibn Battuta about 75,000 miles from 1325 to about 1352 (World History Encyclopedia), Zheng He reaching East Africa on the 1413 to 1415 voyage (Britannica).
- hp04: "the Inca built over 20,000 miles of roads": Wikipedia gives about 40,000 km but says estimates range from 23,000 to 60,000 km (about 14,000 to 37,000 miles) → changed to "more than 10,000 miles", which every estimate supports. `real` said storms caused both failures; the 1274 storm is debated → now "in 1281 a huge typhoon sank much of their fleet". Mansa Musa in 1324 and gold losing value: confirmed in al-Umari's account.
- hp06: `real` said "about two years" for Aug 1911 to Dec 1913 → "a bit over two years". The lighthouse source (lighthousefriends ID 581) is a dead page → replaced with New World Encyclopedia ("functioned as a lighthouse from 1886 to 1902"). The 1966 trophy found by Pickles and the Harvard moth in 1947 both check out.
- hp07: "Abraham Lincoln is in the National Wrestling Hall of Fame": Snopes says he was honored with its 1992 Outstanding American award → reworded to "is honored in". The cap "George Washington's false teeth were carved out of wood" is a widely debunked myth that savvy players spot (the writer flagged it) → replaced with the less-known myth "Napoleon's soldiers shot the nose off Egypt's Great Sphinx". Smithsonian Journeys: Frederic Norden's 1737 drawings already show no nose, and Napoleon was born in 1769 and reached Egypt in 1798. Franklin's swim fins at 11 (Franklin Institute) and Elizabeth II's ATS mechanic training in 1945 (Snopes) both check out.
- hp08: "a melted candy bar led to the invention of the microwave oven": Wikipedia calls the candy bar story a legend → replaced with "Silly Putty came from a World War II attempt to make fake rubber" (James Wright at GE in 1943, on a government contract for a rubber substitute, per Wikipedia). Bubble wrap as wallpaper (Smithsonian), the Slinky's naval mechanical engineer (Wikipedia) and Velcro from burrs in 1941 (History.com) all check out.
- hp09: the cap "King Tut's tomb was found empty" was too easy, since Tut's gold treasure is famous → new cap "King Tut's tomb was the first royal tomb found in the Valley of the Kings". It's definitely false: Belzoni found Seti I's tomb on Oct 16, 1817, and Tut's is KV62, found in 1922. `real` and the source were rewritten. "Machu Picchu was built for an Inca emperor around 1450" → "in the 1400s", to avoid pinning an exact date (World History Encyclopedia gives about 1450 under Pachacuti). Eiffel Tower kept for radio (official site, 20-year permit) and Pisa leaning during construction both check out.
- hp11: the cap "Columbus sailed west to prove that the Earth was round" is a well-known myth (the writer flagged it) → replaced with "Columbus sailed home from his first voyage aboard the Santa Maria". It's definitely false: Britannica says the Santa María ran aground on Dec 25, 1492, and the return was made on the Niña. Also checked: Seaman, the 1507 Waldseemüller map, and Magellan's 5 ships with 1 returning.
- hp12: "the first person charged with speeding": the Historic UK source calls Walter Arnold (1896, 8 mph in a 2 mph zone) "the first person to be caught speeding in a motorised vehicle", and earlier speeding offences existed for carriages → reworded to "the first person caught speeding in a car". Daguerre's c. 1838 photo of a man having his boots cleaned (Guinness) and the 1868 gas-lit London traffic light both check out.
